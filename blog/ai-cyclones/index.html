Can AI forecast tropical cyclones?

Companion to the paper Can AI Forecast the Extremes? Evaluating Probabilistic Weather Models on Tropical Cyclones, by Nathan Chalumeau, Xavier Renard, Remi Meynadier, Hugo Rakotoarimanga, Marcin Detyniecki and Emmanuel de Bézenac.

AI has been driving a small revolution in weather prediction. These models are orders of magnitude faster to run and, on many standard comparisons, more accurate than the best physics-based forecasts (GraphCast, GenCast).

But I've also seen a lot of skepticism: these models only learn correlations, they don't understand the physics, so why should we expect them to work in conditions they haven't seen before? Studies in Science Advances and PNAS have reported serious limitations in AI forecasts of extreme weather.

Those studies tested deterministic models, which predict a single future and tend to smooth out uncertain features, including the strongest winds. Newer probabilistic models produce several possible futures, each of which can retain a distinct storm. We wanted to know whether they still had the same limitations.

We chose tropical cyclones to test whether improvements on global weather scores extend to extremes. Across 178 cyclones from 2021–2023 and 2025, we examined tracks, intensity, and forecast uncertainty.

1The benchmark

Much of the earlier evaluation focused on deterministic forecasts and metrics. With an ensemble, asking how far one predicted track lies from the observed track is not enough. We also need to ask whether the forecast assigns sensible probabilities to the possible outcomes. Two ensembles can have the same average track and very different uncertainty.

So we built an evaluation framework combining probabilistic scores with cyclone-specific diagnostics. We measure the probability assigned to the observed track, whether storms are detected at all, and where the strongest winds and lowest pressures occur. We also check intensity, whether ensemble spread matches forecast errors, and the relationship between wind and pressure.

Here we'll focus on the 2025 operational forecasts: GenCast and FGN from Google DeepMind, compared with ECMWF's IFS-ENS.

We expected to find substantial limitations compared with physics-based models. Cyclones occupy only about 0.5% of grid points in global climate simulations. It didn't turn out quite how we expected.

3The comparison across storms

Of the 53 storms selected in 2025, 41 met the track-scoring criteria: all three models tracked them in at least 10 members at five or more common times. Each storm has one forecast start, 6 to 12 hours before its first reported wind observation.

IFS-ENS has the lowest track NLL of all three models on three storms: Taliah, Garance, and Errol. All three are in the South Indian Ocean.

These are substantial gains against a leading operational ensemble. The advantage extends beyond storm centres: in the paper, we find that FGN also places the regions of strongest winds and lowest pressures more accurately. Both operational AI models improve more on IFS-ENS in cyclone regions than they do globally, in relative CRPS terms.

This is what surprised me: in this evaluation, the relative gains are larger around cyclones than globally. The concern about extremes is reasonable, but these results give us reasons to reconsider it.

4The winds are still too weak

Better tracks do not mean that every aspect of the storm is better predicted. The AI models’ winds are still too weak.

Part of the gap with observations comes from resolution. A grid spaced roughly 25 km apart smooths the narrow region of strongest winds around the eye, lowering the peak even when the storm is correctly located.

But the AI models also underestimate winds relative to the gridded operational analysis used in our evaluation. Resolution does not explain the whole bias.

Their intensity distributions remain close to ERA5, the data used for pretraining, even after fine-tuning on operational analyses. ERA5 itself underestimates cyclone intensity. We suspect this reflects the influence of pretraining, though we would need to change the training data to test that explanation. Would introducing operational data earlier help?

We also wanted to see where FGN might fail. Its loss does not directly enforce the joint distribution across variables: it could predict wind and pressure well separately without getting their relationship right. In cyclones, stronger winds generally accompany lower central pressure. Does FGN reproduce that?

Once again, the empirical result was more encouraging than we expected. After accounting for biases in wind and pressure separately, FGN reproduces a dependence similar to the reference data, as does GenCast. The storms are too weak, but the wind–pressure relationship is retained, even without being directly enforced by the loss.

Which would you trust more: a model that gave the better forecast for 90% of past storms, or one that “respects the physics”? And what if the next storm is unlike any of them?

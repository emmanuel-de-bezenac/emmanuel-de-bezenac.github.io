Where will the storm go? AI weather models and the tropical cyclones of 2025

Companion to the paper Can AI Forecast the Extremes? Evaluating Probabilistic Weather Models on Tropical Cyclones, joint work with Nathan Chalumeau and co-authors

AI weather models now match or beat the best physics-based forecasts on average scores. Whether they also work for rare, extreme events, where forecasts matter most, is still debated.

In our paper we compare three AI ensemble models (GenCast, FourCastNet 3 and FGN) with ECMWF's operational physics-based ensemble, IFS-ENS, on 178 tropical cyclones between 2021 and 2025. This page shows the 2025 part of that comparison, using the forecasts themselves. It covers GenCast, FGN and IFS-ENS, in the versions that run operationally.

In short
  1. The AI models forecast the track better. FGN gives the observed storm positions more probability than IFS-ENS on 37 of the 41 storms of 2025, GenCast on 32.
  2. They make the storms too weak. Both underestimate the maximum wind by about 15 to 20% between 2 and 9 days ahead; IFS-ENS stays within about 10%.
  3. The weak storms still behave like cyclones. Once the difference in intensity is removed, wind depends on pressure in the same way in every model as in the observations.
To score an ensemble of 49 tracks we treat it as a probability distribution (section 2). But first, the forecasts themselves.

The three ensembles send the storms along similar paths. The colours differ: the AI members are usually paler than the observed track and than IFS-ENS, because their winds are weaker. Switch to Rescaled to observations to remove that difference and compare the tracks alone.

2Scoring a forecast made of 49 storms

An ensemble does not give one track. It gives 49 possible positions at each time. Earlier evaluations average the members into a mean track and measure its distance to the observed storm. This discards the spread: two ensembles with the same mean can differ completely, and when the members split into two groups the mean can fall between them, where no member is.

We score the ensemble as a probability distribution instead. The member positions are smoothed into a probability density, and the score is minus the logarithm of that density at the observed position: the negative log-likelihood, or NLL. Lower is better. A forecast scores badly when it is confident and wrong, and also when it is spread so widely that it says little. The two examples below are times at which the two scores disagree: the model whose mean is closest to the storm has the highest NLL.

3The 2025 season: 41 storms

We scored every 2025 storm that reached 64 kt and that all three models tracked in at least 10 members at 5 or more common times: 41 storms in total. For each storm, the NLL is averaged over these times.

No model is best everywhere: IFS-ENS has the lowest NLL on three storms, and the order of FGN and GenCast changes from storm to storm.

Compare two models storm by storm

The ranking does not depend on how far ahead the forecast is. Below, each scored time of each storm counts once, grouped by forecast day.

From day 1 to day 10, FGN has the lowest NLL at 45 to 60% of the scored times, GenCast at 33 to 48%, and IFS-ENS at 4 to 13%. After day 10 few storms are still tracked by all three models, so those columns rest on a few cases.

4The right place, but too weak

The damage a cyclone does depends on its intensity as much as on its position. Here the AI models are weaker than the reference. Compared with HRES-fc0, the ECMWF analysis on which both were fine-tuned, FGN and GenCast underestimate the maximum 10 m wind by about 15 to 20% between 2 and 9 days of lead time. IFS-ENS stays within about 10%. The AI models' bias is close to that of ERA5, the older reanalysis on which they were first trained. This suggests that the training data play a role, but our evaluation does not establish it.

Do the weak storms still behave like cyclones? In a cyclone, lower central pressure goes with stronger wind. Below, each source contributes the minimum pressure and maximum wind of every member at every time. The AI models and ERA5 sit at weaker winds and higher pressures than HRES-fc0 and the observations. Replacing each value by its rank within its own dataset removes these differences in intensity. What remains is how wind varies with pressure, and it is nearly the same for every source.

The models therefore reproduce how wind depends on pressure, and their error is in intensity. This holds for FGN, whose training loss scores each variable at each grid point separately and does not constrain how variables depend on each other.

The same ranks put the forecast winds on the observed scale, which is what "Rescaled to observations" does in the player of section 1. For each source, the curves below give the share of its 2025 maximum winds that are below each speed. A member's wind is moved from its model's curve to the observed curve at the same height: it gets the observed wind with the same rank. The strongest winds of a model become the strongest observed winds, the median becomes the observed median, and so on. Choose a model and move the pointer over the chart.

5What this shows and what it does not

  • Each storm has one forecast start time, about 12 hours before its first observation. Forecasts started closer to landfall are more accurate for every model.
  • Members in which the tracker does not find the storm are not scored. The paper reports the detection rate separately; on average it is similar for FGN and GenCast and lower for IFS-ENS in 2025.
  • NLL has no upper bound. A single time at which the storm is far from every member can dominate a storm's average. This is the case for GenCast on Typhoon Halong.
  • 2025 is one season. In 2021–2023, with the models trained on ERA5, GenCast, FourCastNet 3 and IFS-ENS were much closer to each other.
  • Reproducing a physical relationship such as the dependence of wind on pressure does not show that a model has learned the underlying physics.

The paper has the full evaluation: forecast skill in the fields around the storm, the location of the strongest winds, ensemble calibration, and the 2021–2023 comparison with FourCastNet 3. Read it on arXiv.
